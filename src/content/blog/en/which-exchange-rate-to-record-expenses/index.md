Which rate do you use?

At the end of my first trip, I got stuck on that question. I'd spent euros in Vienna, forints in Budapest, złoty in Kraków, korunas in Prague. I needed it all in my own currency.

But at which day's rate?

Before the trip, I'd heard that banks convert card spending at a high rate. So I asked for my card statement in euros. The plan was simple: at the end of the trip, I'd buy the euros myself and pay it off.

By then, the euro had already gone up. I had to pay more than I'd expected.

The day you spend and the day the money actually leaves your pocket aren't always the same day.

Years later, trying to track several currencies at once, I understood that the day behind a rate can completely change what a country cost me.

## Two ways

### At today's rate

You convert all your past spending at the rate of the day you're looking. Easy: one rate does it all.

But every time you look, the past changes. As the rate moves, the "amount" of the rent you paid six months ago moves too. Without you doing anything, your past turns into a different number.

### At the rate of the day you paid

You record each expense at the rate of the day you paid it. A bit more work: every expense has its own rate.

But once it's recorded, the past stays put. The rent you paid in January is still the rent you paid in January when you look in June.

## Accountants answered this long ago

Companies have had the same problem for decades, and the rule is clear.

Under IAS 21, the international accounting standard, a transaction in a foreign currency is first recorded at **the spot rate on the date of the transaction**. At the end of each period, money you hold and other monetary items are revalued at **the closing rate**.

Two questions, two rates:

- **What did I spend?** Something that happened in the past. At that day's rate.
- **What do I have?** Today's value. At today's rate.

I can't see why a personal budget should be any different.

## Six months in numbers

Let's not use my own numbers. Let's use a round example. Rates move differently every period; the point is to see the size of it.

You spent six months in a country and paid **500** in rent in the local currency every month. In January, 1 unit of that currency was worth **1.00** in your main currency. Over the six months it slowly weakened, down to **0.80** by June.

- **At the rate of the day you paid:** January 500, February 480, March 460, April 440, May 420, June 400. Total: **2,700**.
- **At today's (June's) rate:** 6 × 500 × 0.80 = **2,400**.

The **300** in between is a discount nobody ever gave you. Someone looking at today's rate believes the stay cost them 11% less. But the rent was paid every month at that month's rate. The 2,700 really left their pocket.

Now flip it: had the local currency strengthened, today's rate would make the past look more expensive than it was. Either way, what's wrong isn't the country. It's the maths.

And one more thing: a spreadsheet kept at today's rate shows a different total when you open it tomorrow. No expense changed. When you're trying to compare two countries, the ground moves under your feet.

## What I do now

**I record every expense at the rate of the day I paid.** When I built Nomad Budget, this was the one rule I wouldn't negotiate: every entry is stored at the rate of the day it was paid, and everything adds up in the main currency you choose. Each country's wallet stays in its own currency: lari are lari, złoty are złoty.

**I always enter it the same day.** It's a habit now. The rate is right, and no coffee gets forgotten by the evening.

**I keep the two questions apart.** "What did this country cost me?" at the rates of those days. "What do I have right now?" at today's rate.

The question I asked at the end of my first trip looked simple. The answer turned out simple too: whatever happened in the past happened at that day's rate.

I wish you a budget that keeps the past exactly as it was.
