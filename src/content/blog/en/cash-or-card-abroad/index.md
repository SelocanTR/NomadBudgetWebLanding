Is your card enough?

When I first started travelling, I thought my card would handle everything. Then I held it out and heard the same words.

Cash only.

It happened more often in those early days. Luckily, I never ended up unable to pay. That's why I always try to keep some cash on me, just for that moment.

I've had the opposite too: realising I was about to leave a country with a pocket full of its cash. That money gets spent in a rush on the last day, or changed back at a bad rate.
Cash or card? There's no single answer.

But every country has a right mix.

## Even in Europe, cash isn't gone

According to the European Central Bank's 2024 study, **52%** of in-store payments in the euro area are still made in cash. In 2022 it was 59%. Falling, but not gone. By value, cash is 39%.

The gap between countries is big. In Malta, 67% of in-store payments are cash; in Slovenia, 64%. In the Netherlands it's 22%, in Finland 27%.

The smaller the amount, the bigger the role of cash: 68% of payments of €5 or less are cash, and more than half of those between €5 and €30.

And one more: 24% of euro area consumers said that in the past month, the payment method they wanted wasn't always accepted.

So even inside Europe, you need a different wallet depending on the country and the amount. Outside Europe, the gap is often bigger.

## What cash costs

### Withdrawing

Every withdrawal means an ATM fee and the risk of a bad rate. I covered them in detail in [my post on card fees and DCC](/blog/exchange-rates-card-fees-dcc/). In short: withdraw rarely and in bigger amounts, try another bank if the screen shows a fee, and say no to "your home currency."

### Limits

Your card has a daily withdrawal limit, and the ATM may have its own. If you've rented a flat where the rent is paid in cash, that limit can suddenly matter. Checking your limit with your bank before you go beats being surprised at an ATM on day one.

### Carrying it

Lost cash doesn't come back. A lost card gets blocked.

### What's left

The cash you still have when you leave a country is the quietest loss of all. Changing it back, you pay the spread again. Coins, in many places, you can't change at all. A drawer of foreign coins is spending that never happened.

## What cards cost

Cards have a price too: the exchange margin, and with some banks a foreign transaction fee. But with a card there's no "took out too much and kept it." You pay for what you spend.

The real catch: where the card isn't accepted, it costs nothing. And it's useless.

## One month in numbers

Let's not use my own numbers. Let's use a round example. Fees depend on the bank, the ATM and the country; the point is to see the size of it.

Say the things you have to pay in cash come to **150 units** a month: the market, the bakery, small shops, transport.

- **Withdraw 25 units whenever you need it:** 6 withdrawals × 5 units in fees = **30 units**
- **Withdraw 150 units once, at the start of the month:** 1 withdrawal × 5 units = **5 units**

One big withdrawal saves 25 units. But there's a trap: take out too much, and you leave the country with money in your pocket.

- At the end of the month, 40 units of cash are left. Changing them back loses 10%: **4 units**
- 5 units of it are coins you can't change at all: **5 units**

One big withdrawal still wins: 5 + 4 + 5 = 14 units, less than half of the 30 from withdrawing bit by bit. But if you forget the leftover cash, you give back part of what you thought you'd saved on the last day.

## What I do now

**I keep only enough cash for emergencies.** I try not to change much.

**Leftover cash stays in the country.** If some is left, I tip people a little more, or pay in cash on my last days.

**I check how much cash a country runs on first.** Before I go, I look up whether it's mostly cash or card.

**Cash spending goes in the same wallet.** Card payments leave a trail. The tomatoes paid in cash at the market don't. If I didn't put everything I pay in cash into that country's wallet in Nomad Budget, part of what a country really costs would never show.

That "cash only" moment still comes now and then. Now there's enough cash in my pocket for it — but not enough to end up in a drawer when I leave.

I wish you the right country's money in your pocket, every time.
