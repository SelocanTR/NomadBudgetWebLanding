What did that trip really cost me?

At the end of my first long trip, the number in my head didn't match. Vienna, Budapest, Kraków, Prague: four cities, four currencies. Part of the gap had gone to exchange rates, part to fees.

But what I really noticed that day was something else. There was no simple way to do this sum properly.

Years later, living as a digital nomad, the same question came back. Every month.

## Three attempts

### Not tracking at all

At first, I didn't track my spending at all. At the end of the month I looked at what was left in the account. I didn't know what a country cost me. I only knew there was less money after it.

### Keeping it in my head

Then I tried to keep it in my head. Rent is this much, groceries roughly that, cafés a bit more than they should be.

It didn't work.

Hard enough in one currency. Adding euros and lari, złoty and lira in the same head made it impossible. Every month, the number in my head was either too optimistic or not there at all.

### Expense apps

Finally, I tried expense apps. Almost every one of them had plenty of gaps. None of them fully met what a digital nomad needs.

## Three questions that need answers

Whatever method you use, if you live in several currencies, there are three questions you have to answer every day.

### Which rate?

At what rate do you convert each expense? Add everything up at today's rate, and your past changes every day. Use the rate of the day you paid, and you need that day's rate for every expense. I wrote about this in [a separate post](/blog/which-exchange-rate-to-record-expenses/).

### Which country?

If you want to see what a country cost you, you have to split spending by country. Moving day. A week between two countries. Fixed costs still running back home. All of it has to belong somewhere.

### Which total?

Euros, lari, złoty, lira. You want to see it all in one currency. But you also need to see each one in its own. Two totals, two truths.

A head can't carry all three questions at once. An app can — but only if it was built for them.

And there was one more thing: it shouldn't be only numbers. Some feeling had to come into it too.

That's how Nomad Budget came about.

## The rules I wouldn't negotiate

When I built the app, there were a few rules I never bent:

**One wallet per country, in its own currency.** Lari are lari, złoty are złoty. Everything you spent in a country stays there, in that country's money.

**Every entry at the rate of the day it was paid.** The past doesn't change with the day you look at it.

**One total, in the currency you choose.** It all comes together in one number, in the main currency you pick.

**Where you've been, and what it cost, on a globe.** I said it shouldn't be only numbers; the globe is the feeling part. I wanted to create a visual memory: the countries you've crossed, the route you've drawn, what each country cost you, all in one place. Look at a list and you see your spending. Look at the globe and you remember the journey.

**No bank connection, no data sold.** Location is read only at country level, and never stored. A budget app doesn't need to get into your bank to know you.

**It works offline.** I wrote in [my post on the first week](/blog/first-week-in-a-new-country/) that your first hours in a new country are often without internet. You still spend money in those hours.

The question I asked at the end of my first trip was simple: what did this journey cost me? Nomad Budget exists to answer it every day, in every country, at a glance.

I wish you a budget that crosses borders with you.
