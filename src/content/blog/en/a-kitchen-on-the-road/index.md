Do you cook when you travel?

I do. And I eat out. Both.

On a two-day trip, you eat out. When you live somewhere for one, three or six months, a kitchen becomes part of the deal. And the supermarket becomes part of the city.

The countries I live in change my cooking, too. The way I cook has changed, and so have the spice combinations I use. Even the way I cook eggs isn't what it used to be.

## The first shop

The first thing I always do in a new place is a grocery run, so the basics are at hand.

A rented kitchen always lacks something. There's a pan, but no salt. A stove, but no oil. I wrote about the rest of that first week in [my post on the first week in a new country](/blog/first-week-in-a-new-country/).

This one is genuinely interesting, and it's something you only learn on the road. At the start, I'd never have thought I'd carry things like this. But I do. Some spices I like, my favourite coffee mug, my teapot, sometimes even my spatula.

As soon as I arrive in a country, I buy breakfast things. Food you can eat right away, without much cooking, is a real advantage. Then, once I've learned where things are fresh and where there's more choice, I start doing my cooking shop at those places.

Even working out which brands are good can take time. With products I don't know, I always buy the small size. And if I'm not sure what's in something, I sometimes just skip it.

## Reading a country through its supermarket

A supermarket tells you a lot about a place: what people eat, what's local, what's imported, what's cheap and what's a luxury.

In some countries, like Kazakhstan, I was surprised to see supermarkets still selling things like bulgur, rice or biscuits loose. But on the whole, supermarkets look alike now.

I'm not a big fan of open-air markets. Still, when I see someone selling fruit and vegetables on the street, I love buying them fresh.

## Not everything is cheap at the same rate

Food prices differ a lot between countries, even inside Europe. According to Eurostat, in 2025 food prices in Romania were 80% of the EU average; in Luxembourg, 122%.

But "cheap country" doesn't mean everything is cheap at the same rate. Eurostat is clear that prices for services differ between countries much more than prices for goods. A meal out is mostly someone's work; a bag of rice is mostly a product. I wrote about this in [my post on geo-arbitrage](/blog/does-geo-arbitrage-work/).

## Eating out, or cooking

All of it plays a part. In an expensive country, I cook at home more often, or pick up ready meals from the supermarket. In cheaper countries, cooking and eating out can sometimes cost about the same. Then I choose eating out.

It's not only about price. When I can't find anything healthy, or anything that suits my taste, I'd rather cook. And when the weather is really bad, I just don't feel like going out.

## What I do now

**The first shop comes first.** Basics at hand, then everything else.

**I eat out and I cook.** The balance changes; the habit doesn't.

**I track each one separately.** In Nomad Budget, groceries, restaurants, cafés and bars each have their own category.

A kitchen turns a flat into a home for a while. And someone selling fresh fruit and veg around the corner turns a neighbourhood into one you don't want to leave.

I wish you someone selling fresh fruit around the corner, wherever you unpack.
