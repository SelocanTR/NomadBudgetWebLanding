How do you get to know a new city?

Guidebooks? Tours? A list of the top ten sights?

I walk.

A map shows you streets. Walking shows you a city: which corners are loud, which are calm, where people sit in the evening, where the bread smells good in the morning.

I really get to know every city I travel to on foot. In a new city, I don't end the day without 15,000 to 20,000 steps. If I'm not in a hurry and not carrying anything heavy, I walk wherever I'm going.

Even if I have to go to the same place again and again, I take different streets each time.

## Day one: the supermarket

The first thing I always do in a new place is a grocery run, so the basics are at hand. I wrote about the rest of that first week in [my post on the first week in a new country](/blog/first-week-in-a-new-country/).

Once the fridge isn't empty, the walking starts.

## Then: every direction

I get to know the area around the flat on foot. A lot of walking, in every direction.

Sometimes I check the map to see what's around. But the map is for checking, not for leading. The best finds are usually the ones I wasn't looking for.

Of course there are places like that. But walking is my routine, so I know I'll find them. In a car or on a bus, we pass by fast and notice very little.

## Follow the crowd

To find a city's central spots, I have a simple strategy: follow the crowd.

Where people go, there's usually a reason. A square, a market, a street where the city comes together. You don't need to know its name. You just need to walk where everyone else is walking.

I do this especially when I've just arrived. Instead of looking at a map, I follow people, and I end up on the city's most central streets, its shopping areas, its social spaces. The rule works everywhere.

## Without headphones

When I walk, I walk without headphones. Just me and my thoughts.

A new city has its own sound: the language on the street, the traffic, the bells, the market. With headphones on, you're walking through your own playlist, not through the city.

## What walking gives back

It's the cheapest way to see a city. It's also how I find the things that make daily life work: the right café, the better supermarket, the bakery that opens early.

Whether I use public transport depends on the country, the city and how far I need to go that day. The weather plays a part too. If there's a metro, I take it. If not, depending on traffic and crowds, I take a bus or a taxi.

Public transport has its own gift: it's a chance to watch the people who live there and see their routines.

## What I do now

**The supermarket comes first.** Basics at hand, then everything else.

**I walk in every direction.** The area around the flat first, then further.

**15,000 to 20,000 steps a day.** If I'm not in a hurry, I walk where I'm going.

**I use the map to check, not to lead.** Sometimes I look at what's around. Mostly, I just walk.

**I follow the crowd to find the centre.** People usually know where the city happens.

A few days of walking, and a new city stops being a map. It becomes streets I know.

I wish you good shoes, and streets worth getting lost in.
