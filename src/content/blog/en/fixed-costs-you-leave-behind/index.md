What are you still paying for at home?

Years ago I wrote about not owning a car. For a life on the move, a car meant being tied to one place, physically and financially, and leaving it to rot in a garage. Why pay for something I can't use for long?

For the car, the answer was easy.

But a car isn't the only thing that ties you down.

When you leave, a lot of small ties stay behind: a phone line, a few subscriptions, a bank account's fees, maybe a storage unit with your things, maybe a home. None of them is big on its own. All of them keep getting paid every month, wherever you are.

I don't close everything either. Some ties I cut, some I shrink, and one I keep on purpose: my home.

## The invisible country

When you work out what a country costs you, you look at what you spent there: rent, groceries, cafés, transport.

But the fixed costs you left behind get paid that month too. They just don't show up in that country's numbers.

In a way, your budget always has one more country in it: the one back home. You don't live there. You pay its rent every month.

### Subscriptions

Streaming, music, cloud storage, the gym you used to go to. Some of them you use on the road. Some you forgot about. The forgotten ones are the most expensive: you get nothing for them.

### The phone line and the bank

You often can't close your home number: your bank's verification codes go to it, some accounts are tied to it. Account fees and card fees don't stop because you're travelling.

### Your things

If it doesn't fit in a suitcase, it's somewhere. At a family home, for free, if you're lucky. In a storage unit, it's one more rent every month.

### A home

If you keep your home, it's the biggest fixed cost of all: rent or mortgage, service charges, bills. And the longer the road gets, the louder the question: should I keep paying for a home I'm not living in?

There's no single answer. For some, it's a weight. For me, it's the place I can always go back to.

## One month in numbers

Let's not use my own numbers. Let's use a round example. The lines and amounts are different for everyone; the point is to see the size of it.

Say life in the country you live in costs **800 units** a month. At home, you'd spend **1,000 units**. On paper, you save 200 a month.

Now add what you left behind:

- Subscriptions: **20 units**
- The phone line at home: **10 units**
- Bank and card fees: **10 units**
- A storage unit for your things: **40 units**

That's **80 units**. Your real monthly cost isn't 800, it's **880**. Your saving isn't 200, it's **120**.

The invisible country eats 40% of the saving you can see. And that's for someone who didn't keep their home.

## What I do now

**The longer the trip, the fewer the ties.** On very long trips, I try not to leave many fixed payments behind.

**On shorter trips, I shrink the phone line.** Instead of closing it, I sometimes switch it to the lowest package.

**I keep my digital subscriptions.** I can use them anywhere, so they travel with me.

**I keep my home.** Having a home I can always go back to makes me feel at ease. So I don't close it, and I keep paying some of its bills. It's a line in my invisible country I pay on purpose.

**I keep home's costs in their own place.** In Nomad Budget, everything I spend in Turkey still goes into my Turkey wallet, wherever I am. That way what the country I'm in costs, and what the invisible country back home costs, show up separately — and together in the total.

The car was an easy answer. It was a big, visible tie. The small ties are sneakier. Each one is small, and each one says: leave it for now.

I wish you things left behind that feel like a safety net, not a weight.
