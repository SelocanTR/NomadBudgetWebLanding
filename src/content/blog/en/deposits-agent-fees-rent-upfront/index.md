How much does a flat cost before you've slept in it?

When you find a local flat, the first joy is the rent. Clearly cheaper than the furnished flats on the platforms. Close to what locals pay.

Then the landlord, or the agent, keeps talking. Deposit. Agent's fee. First month. Sometimes first and last month together.

Before the key is in your hand, you can be several months' rent down.

I've been there.

When I rented a flat in Georgia, they asked for a deposit. Even if you get it back later, paying it upfront is an extra weight.

Luckily, the agent's fee there was paid by the landlord.

## What you pay before you move in

### The deposit

The landlord's security: if something gets damaged or the rent isn't paid, it comes out of here.

The rules vary a lot by country. Germany, for example, sets a legal cap: the deposit can be at most three times the monthly rent excluding service charges, the tenant has the right to pay it in three equal monthly instalments, and the landlord must keep the money in a bank, separate from their own.

Not every country has a cap like that. In some places the deposit is negotiable. In others, it's whatever the landlord says.

### The agent's fee

If you find the flat through an agent, the agent gets paid too. Who pays, and how much, again depends on the country. In some places the landlord pays; in others, the tenant. If it's you, that money doesn't come back — and on a one, three or six-month stay, it has to be shared across the whole stay.

### Rent upfront

Some landlords, especially with foreign and short-term tenants, want several months' rent in advance. It looks like security. For you, it's money locked up before you arrive.

## A deposit is really a currency bet

There's a hidden side to deposits.

You pay it in that country's currency. You get it back months later, often after you've left the country.

In between, two things can happen:

- That country's currency moves against the one you earn in. The same deposit is worth more or less when it comes back. I wrote about this in [my post on currency risk](/blog/earning-in-one-currency-living-in-another/).
- You're in another country by then. The deposit comes back by international transfer, with its own fee and its own rate.

And then there are deductions: cleaning, a bit of damage, a bill that wasn't closed. Getting the whole deposit back is never certain.

## Three months in numbers

Let's not use my own numbers. Let's use a round example. Real figures depend on the country, the flat and the landlord; the point is to see the size of it.

Say you stay three months in a local flat at **500 units** a month.

Before you move in:

- A two-month deposit: **1,000 units**
- An agent's fee of half a month's rent: **250 units**
- The first month's rent: **500 units**

**1,750 units** before you have the key. Three and a half times the monthly rent.

Three months later, the deposit comes back. Not all of it:

- A cleaning deduction: **−50 units**
- That country's currency fell 5% in the meantime: **−50 units**
- An international transfer fee: **−10 units**

Of the 1,000-unit deposit, 890 come back. The 110 that don't, plus the 250 agent's fee, add **360 units** to a three-month stay. That's 120 a month — **24%** of the rent.

That number may sound familiar. In [my post on the short-term rent premium](/blog/short-term-rent-premium/) I explained why furnished platform flats cost more than local rent. On short stays, that premium can sometimes cost less than a local flat's hidden costs. On long stays it's the other way round: the fee and the losses are shared by more months.

## What I do now

**The length of the stay decides.** For a month, I go with a platform flat. For three or six months, I look for a local one.

**The country matters too.** In some places a local flat is hard to find, because of the language or the contract.

**Sometimes I start on a platform.** I spend the first few weeks in a platform flat, get to know the city, then move to a local one.

**I photograph the flat when I move in.** I usually do it, so there's no trouble later. Most deposit deductions come down to being able to say "it was already like that" when you leave.

**I divide the total cost by the length of the stay.** Not the monthly rent — the cost including deposit losses and fees, divided by the months I stay. In the end, I'm the one who pays all of it.

**The part that doesn't come back counts as that country's spending.** A deposit isn't spending. But the 110 units that never come back are part of living there. If I don't put them in that country's wallet, the country looks cheaper than it was.

A local flat is still often cheaper. But what's cheap is the rent, not the flat. Once you count what you pay before you get the key, which flat is cheaper depends on how long you stay.

I wish you deposits that come back in full, and on time.
